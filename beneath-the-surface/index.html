THE SCIENCE / WITHOUT THE SALES PITCH

WHAT YOU'RE LOOKING AT.

01 / RESEARCH

What we know

Light is absorbed and scattered as it passes through water. Suspended particles and dissolved color change how much light—and which wavelengths—remain. Studies of white crappie and related sunfish provide clues about their visual systems.

02 / ILLUSTRATION

What we're approximating

This demonstration uses example clarity settings, a simplified three-channel light attenuation calculation, illustrative bait colors and stylized viewing palettes. It is not calibrated to a particular lake, bait material or actual fish retina.

03 / LIMITS

What we don't claim

We are not claiming to show exactly what a crappie experiences—or promising which color catches fish. Depth is a visual timeline, not a prediction of lure action. No bait recommendations, rigging guidance or bite forecasts live in this exhibit.

VIEW MODEL ASSUMPTIONS AND LIMITATIONS +

For illustration, relative light in three broad color channels is reduced with depth using I(z) = I(0) × exp(−k × z). The coefficients are illustrative, not measured for Grenada, Enid, Sardis, or any other lake. Each water preset uses different example attenuation coefficients and a water-color/scattering treatment; the app does not calculate a full underwater radiative-transfer solution.

Weather changes the example amount and character of incoming daylight. Selected bait colors are artistic approximations, not measured reflectance spectra. Fish mode transforms the resulting colors into an intentionally different, green/red-sensitive illustrative palette inspired by research in related sunfish. Since the specific crappie spectral sensitivity and perceptual appearance are not established here, this should be read as one possible visualization, not proof of how a crappie sees.

The animation artificially enlarges the bait and adjusts scene exposure so visitors can observe color changes. Contrast and apparent visibility do not imply a measured detection range or a fish's response. A fully quantitative comparison would require site measurements, bait reflectance testing, viewing geometry and validated visual physiology.
